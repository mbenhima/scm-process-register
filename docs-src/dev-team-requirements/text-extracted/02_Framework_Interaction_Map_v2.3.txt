1. Introduction

1.1 Why journi Tracks Four Frameworks at Once

Most change-management tools pick one framework and force everything
through it. journi deliberately does not, because no single framework
answers every question a Change Manager actually needs answered. Each of
the four frameworks covered in this document was built by a different
author, for a different purpose, at a different unit of analysis — and
journi treats them as four lenses on the same underlying reality rather
than four competing methodologies to choose between.

1.2 What's New in This Edition

This edition expands each of the four frameworks with the specific,
observable signs a Change Manager should look for at every stage —
verbal, behavioral, and data signals, not just definitions. It then
applies the full interaction map to a concrete, vendor-agnostic ERP
implementation using two operational tools borrowed from process design:
SIPOC (Suppliers – Inputs – Process – Outputs – Customers) to define
what happens and who touches it, and RACSI (Responsible – Accountable –
Consulted – Support – Informed) to define who does what. Section 5 gives
the detailed normal flow across all eight implementation phases; Section
6 gives detailed exception flows for the typical ways that normal flow
breaks down. Both sections carry a timeline and a narrative simulation
of how the underlying change-management dynamic actually behaves.

This revision adds three further layers. First, throughout Sections 5
and 6, every SIPOC process step and every RACSI step row is explicitly
tagged as Project Management work, Change Management work, or Joint
work, so the two disciplines' contributions to a single implementation
are visually and analytically distinguishable rather than blended into
one undifferentiated task list. Second, Section 2's signal catalogues
are expanded and every signal is given a Signal ID and classified Must
Have (a core, independently reliable indicator) or Nice to Have (a
corroborating, contextual indicator) — 127 signals in total, 69 Must
Have and 58 Nice to Have. Third, every signal is explicitly mapped to
the Project/Change task that captures it, together with a technique
(survey, interview, focus group, telemetry review, and so on) and an
open-source or free tool to run it with; Section 7 confirms that this
mapping reaches 100% coverage of every signal in the catalogue, adding
one new dedicated signal-capture task per phase where the existing flow
left a gap.

1.3 How to Read This Document

Section 2 details each framework stage-by-stage, including the signal
catalogue, the Must Have / Nice to Have classification, and the
task/technique/tool mapping. Section 3 explains why journi uses four
frameworks instead of one. Section 4 is the interaction map itself — the
master correspondence table, the pairwise breakdown, and journi's two
computed metrics. Section 5 is the applied normal flow for an ERP
implementation, with the Project/Change tagging and the technique/tool
detail described above. Section 6 is the applied exception flows, tagged
the same way. Section 7 is the signal coverage matrix confirming 100%
coverage. Section 8 gives a decision framework for reading all four
together in practice, and Section 9 summarizes the worked examples.
Section 10 catalogues the ten Change Management macro processes
(Suppliers/Inputs/ID/Name/Detailed Tasks/Outputs/Beneficiaries plus a
five-column RACSI). Section 11 chains those macro processes into four
end-to-end processes, each with its own SIPOC and five-column RACSI. The
appendix is a quick-reference glossary plus the
SIPOC/RACSI/Project-Change/Must-Nice legends.

2. The Four Frameworks, In Detail — With Signs to Look For

Each framework below is presented with its full stage set, what each
stage means, and the concrete signs a Change Manager should watch for at
each stage. These signs are drawn from three sources of evidence: what
people say (verbal signals), what people do (behavioral signals), and
what the data shows (metrics and system signals). None of the signs
listed is individually diagnostic; the pattern across several signs,
corroborated by more than one source, is what should drive a framework
score in journi.

2.1 Lewin's Unfreeze–Change–Refreeze

Author / origin: Kurt Lewin, 1947. Altitude: Organizational / systemic —
the whole population in aggregate.

Lewin is the coarsest-grained and oldest of the four frameworks. It does
not describe an individual's journey; it describes the state of the
organizational system as a whole, and it moves only when enough of the
underlying population has genuinely moved — never on the strength of a
project plan alone.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Stage          Meaning
  -------------- --------------------------------------------------------
  Unfreeze       Building the case for change and deliberately loosening
                 attachment to the status quo. The organization must feel
                 that the current equilibrium is no longer viable before
                 it will let go of it.

  Change         The transition itself. Old patterns have been let go but
                 new ones are not yet habitual; the system is actively
                 moving between two equilibria.

  Refreeze       The new state is deliberately stabilized and reinforced
                 until it becomes simply “the way things are done,” not
                 “the new way.”
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

Unfreeze

Building the case for change and deliberately loosening attachment to
the status quo. The organization must feel that the current equilibrium
is no longer viable before it will let go of it.

Entry signal: A credible, sponsor-backed case for change is first
communicated to the population (a town hall, a memo, a kickoff), even if
informally.

Exit signal: A visible majority of the population accepts that change is
happening and stops actively defending the status quo — even if they
have not yet built any new capability.

Change

The transition itself. Old patterns have been let go but new ones are
not yet habitual; the system is actively moving between two equilibria.

Entry signal: Old-process usage begins measurably declining and
new-process usage begins measurably rising — the shift is observable in
system data, not just sentiment.

Exit signal: New behaviors are performed consistently, without heavy
coaching, across a majority of the population, for a sustained period.

Refreeze

The new state is deliberately stabilized and reinforced until it becomes
simply “the way things are done,” not “the new way.”

Entry signal: New-process metrics stabilize at or above baseline for
multiple consecutive reporting cycles, not a single good week.

Exit signal: N/A — Refreeze is the terminal state for a given change; it
exits only when a new change cycle begins.

Signal catalogue — 10 signals (5 Must Have · 5 Nice to Have)

  -------------------------------------------------------------------------
  ID         Class / Evidence Signal
  ---------- ---------------- ---------------------------------------------
  L-REF-01   [MUST HAVE]      The new process is spontaneously cited as
             (Verbal)         “how we do things” without qualification.

  L-REF-02   [MUST HAVE]      KPIs have stabilized at or above baseline
             (Data)           across multiple consecutive reporting cycles,
                              not a single good week.

  L-REF-03   [MUST HAVE]      Onboarding materials, job aids, and new-hire
             (Behavioral)     training have been updated to reflect the new
                              state as default.

  L-REF-04   [MUST HAVE]      Old-process documentation and system access
             (Data)           have been formally retired, not just
                              deprioritized.

  L-REF-05   [MUST HAVE]      Routine audits, controls, or compliance
             (Data)           checks pass consistently against the new
                              process.

  L-REF-06   [NICE TO HAVE]   Peer-to-peer teaching of the new process
             (Behavioral)     happens without central prompting.

  L-REF-07   [NICE TO HAVE]   The change survives a management or team-lead
             (Behavioral)     turnover without regression.

  L-REF-08   [NICE TO HAVE]   New hires describe the process as simply “the
             (Verbal)         process,” with no awareness a change ever
                              happened.

  L-REF-09   [NICE TO HAVE]   Support-ticket volume for the new process
             (Data)           falls to steady-state / business-as-usual
                              levels.

  L-REF-10   [NICE TO HAVE]   The process appears unprompted in externally
             (Data)           published materials (customer-facing docs,
                              audit reports) as the standard way of
                              working.

2.2 Prosci ADKAR

Author / origin: Prosci. Altitude: Individual / cohort.

ADKAR operates at the level of a specific person or cohort and is
explicitly sequential — each block is a precondition for the next.
Building Desire in someone who lacks Awareness rarely works, and
Knowledge delivered before Desire exists is usually wasted training
spend. journi scores all five blocks 1–5 per project, each requiring a
barrier-reason note when the score is 2 or below (auto-escalated).

  ------------------------------------------------------------------------
  Stage           Meaning
  --------------- --------------------------------------------------------
  Awareness       Does the person understand why the change is happening —
                  the business reason, the risk of not changing, and what
                  specifically will be different for them?

  Desire          Do they personally want to support and participate in
                  the change — not just understand it intellectually?

  Knowledge       Do they know how to change — the specific skills, steps
                  and information required to operate in the future state?

  Ability         Can they actually demonstrate the new skills and
                  behaviors under real working conditions, not just in a
                  training room?

  Reinforcement   Are there mechanisms in place — recognition, metrics,
                  coaching, consequences — to make the change durable once
                  initial attention fades?
  ------------------------------------------------------------------------

Awareness

Does the person understand why the change is happening — the business
reason, the risk of not changing, and what specifically will be
different for them?

Signal catalogue — 10 signals (6 Must Have · 4 Nice to Have)

  -------------------------------------------------------------------------
  ID         Class / Evidence Signal
  ---------- ---------------- ---------------------------------------------
  A-AWA-01   [MUST HAVE]      Employees can restate the business case
             (Verbal)         unprompted, in their own words.

  A-AWA-02   [MUST HAVE]      A drop in “why are we doing this?” questions
             (Data)           after initial communications go out.

2.3 Bridges' Transition Model

Author / origin: William Bridges, Managing Transitions, 1991. Altitude:
Psychological / identity.

Bridges is explicitly psychological rather than procedural: it tracks
the identity journey a person goes through, on its own clock,
independent of the technical or organizational rollout. Even a positive
change can trigger genuine grief for what is being left behind.

  -----------------------------------------------------------------------
  Stage          Meaning
  -------------- --------------------------------------------------------
  Ending         Letting go of the old way — and the identity, competence
                 and relationships built around it — often accompanied by
                 real loss, resistance, or denial, even when the change
                 is objectively positive.

  Neutral Zone   The uncomfortable in-between: old ways no longer apply,
                 but new ways are not yet confident or automatic. This is
                 genuinely disorienting, not simply a delay.

  New Beginning  Genuine buy-in and a new identity forming around the new
                 way of working — not mere compliance, but ownership.
  -----------------------------------------------------------------------

1969. Altitude: Emotional / moment-to-moment.

Where Bridges describes the structure of a transition, Kübler-Ross
describes the emotional weather inside it — a finer-grained,

3. Why Four Frameworks and Not One

Beyond altitude, the four frameworks differ in a second important way:
how strictly ordered they are.

-   Sequential / prescriptive — Prosci ADKAR is genuinely prescriptive:
    the five blocks are meant to be built in sequence, and journi's
    escalation logic (a block scoring ≤2 gets flagged) treats an
    out-of-order or stalled block as an actionable problem, not just a
    data point.

-   Descriptive, organization-level — Lewin is a descriptive
    macro-state, not a checklist: an organization does not “complete
    tasks” to move from Unfreeze to Change the way an individual
    completes ADKAR blocks; it shifts because enough of the underlying
    population has moved.

-   Descriptive, subjective-experience — Bridges and Kübler-Ross
    describe subjective experience. They have a natural order but,
    unlike ADKAR, journi does not enforce that order or flag “going
    backward” as an error: regression to an earlier emotional stage is
    common and expected, especially after a setback, and is itself
    useful diagnostic information rather than a data-entry mistake.

The Practical Upshot

Because the four frameworks operate at different altitudes and with
different degrees of prescriptiveness, none of them can stand in for the
others. A Change Manager who only tracks Lewin sees the forest but not
the trees — and completely misses whether any individual can actually do
the new job. A Change Manager who only tracks ADKAR sees individual
capability but misses the emotional undercurrent that predicts whether
that capability will actually be used once no one is watching. journi
requires all four precisely so that neither blind spot is possible.

4.2 Pairwise Interactions — the Detail

4.2.1 Lewin ↔ ADKAR: the aggregate-vs-individual relationship

Lewin's phase is not an independent measurement — it is a judgment call
that should be informed by the distribution of ADKAR scores across the
population, not set on its own timeline. A common mistake is letting
Lewin follow the project plan's calendar (declaring “Change” because the
go-live date arrived) rather than the evidence: if ADKAR scores are
still clustered near baseline, calling the phase “Change” is
aspirational, not descriptive. The correct relationship runs one
direction: ADKAR evidence should justify a Lewin phase call, not the
other way around.

4.2.2 ADKAR ↔ Bridges: the capability-vs-identity relationship, and the Divergence Pattern

This is the richest interaction of the four, and the only one journi
encodes as an explicit computed alert. A person can score well on
Knowledge and Ability while still being emotionally in Bridges' Ending —
still grieving what the change cost them. This is the “compliance
without commitment” trap. journi's Divergence Pattern Detector flags
exactly this combination:

hasDivergence = (Knowledge >= 3 AND Ability >= 3) AND (Bridges phase ===
"Ending")

Low Knowledge/Ability paired with Ending is not a divergence — it is a
consistent, if early, reading. The pattern only fires when capability is
objectively strong but the emotional/identity read has not moved at all,
which is exactly the situation most likely to look like a “success” on a
training-completion report while quietly setting up a regression once
reinforcement attention moves elsewhere.

4.2.3 Bridges ↔ Kübler-Ross: two layers of the same emotional territory

Bridges describes the structure of a transition; Kübler-Ross describes
the specific emotional stage within that structure. A person can be in
Bridges' Neutral Zone structurally while still cycling emotionally
through Resistance/Anger on a bad day. When the two readings genuinely
disagree for an extended period, treat that mismatch as a signal to look
closer — most often it means the Bridges reading was updated on schedule
or optimism rather than on fresh evidence.

4.2.4 Lewin ↔ Bridges/Kübler-Ross: the two-clock problem

Lewin runs on the organizational clock, driven by delivery milestones.
Bridges and Kübler-Ross run on the emotional clock, driven by how long
it actually takes a given population to process loss and rebuild
identity. These two clocks routinely run at different speeds. Lewin can
honestly read “Change” or even “Refreeze” while Bridges and Kübler-Ross
still read Ending/Denial or Neutral Zone/Resistance. The fix is not to
wait for the emotional clock to catch up on its own; it is to keep
Reinforcement active for as long as the emotional-layer readings say it
is still needed, even after the Lewin phase has technically moved on.

4.3 How journi Operationalizes the Interaction

Composite Readiness Index — blends ADKAR and Kübler-Ross only

adkarPct = (average of the 5 ADKAR block scores / 5) x 100

sentimentPct = Denial 20, Resistance 40, Exploration 70, Commitment 100
(Kübler-Ross stage)

trainingPct = average completion % across logged curricula

Composite Readiness Index = round( adkarPct*0.50 + sentimentPct*0.25 +
trainingPct*0.25 )

Lewin phase and Bridges phase are not terms in this formula at all; they
remain qualitative overlay and context. Only ADKAR and Kübler-Ross
sentiment are numeric enough, and specific enough, to blend directly.

Divergence Pattern Detector — cross-references ADKAR and Bridges only

Covered in full in Section 4.2.2. This is the one place Bridges enters a
computed check — but as a boolean gate, not a blended numeric term.

Overall Change-Management Dynamic — Simulation

Read end to end, the normal flow simulates one coherent
change-management arc. Months 0–2 are dominated by Denial and a slow
Awareness build — the population has not yet felt anything change, and
the phase's steps are almost entirely Project-tagged discovery work
running ahead of the visible change. Months 3–7 are where the arc is
most fragile: Data Migration & Integration is invisible to end users but
is exactly when Desire is most likely to stall and Resistance/Anger is
most likely to peak, so this is the window where a Change Manager's
attention is most valuable and most often under-resourced — note that
even in this heavily Project-tagged phase, the two Change-tagged steps
(logging barrier reasons, updating the Readiness Index) are the ones
that catch the stall before it compounds. Months 6–9 flip the dynamic as
hands-on Testing and Training give people something concrete to react
to, typically moving Kübler-Ross from Resistance into Exploration even
as Bridges settles into the disorienting Neutral Zone; this is also
where Joint steps first appear in volume, because capability sign-off
and adoption sign-off start to depend on each other. Go-live in Month 9
is a single-day organizational-clock event layered on top of an
emotional clock that does not move that fast — expect a short anxiety
spike, not resolution, and expect the Joint-tagged hypercare-activation
step to be the hinge between the two clocks. Months 9–11 (Hypercare) are
where regression is normal and where the last mile of adoption is
actually won, and Months 11–12+ close the arc — now almost entirely on
Change-tagged steps — only when Reinforcement evidence, not the
calendar, supports calling Refreeze.

dynamics simulation, so that a Change Manager can recognize and respond
to it the same way every time it recurs.

8. Decision Framework: Reading the Four Together

journi never auto-computes a Lewin, ADKAR, Bridges or Kübler-Ross value
from a formula (with the two narrow exceptions in Section 4.3) — these
are judgment calls a Change Manager makes from evidence. The criteria
below are how to make that judgment consistently, and they are the same
criteria the normal-flow and exception-flow simulations in Sections 5
and 6 were built against. All seven are Change-Management judgment calls
by nature — none is delegable to a Project Management schedule or
milestone, which is itself the clearest illustration of the
Project/Change distinction introduced in this edition.

-   1. Match the question to the altitude — ask which framework is
    actually the right instrument before reaching for one:
    organizational state (Lewin), individual capability (ADKAR),
    transition structure (Bridges), or moment-to-moment feeling
    (Kübler-Ross) — rather than defaulting to whichever one you updated
    most recently.

-   2. Let Lewin follow, not lead — Lewin should be set from ADKAR's
    aggregate evidence and from real delivery milestones, never from the
    calendar alone (Sections 4.2.1, 4.2.4, and Exception E3).

-   3. ADKAR blocks need evidence, not estimates — a stalled ADKAR block
    needs a written barrier reason, not just a number; the reason is
    what makes the score actionable for the next reviewer (Exception
    E1).

-   4. Cross-check ADKAR against Bridges before declaring capability
    “done” — strong capability with a stuck emotional/identity reading
    is the Divergence Pattern, and it is invisible if you only ever look
    at ADKAR in isolation (Exception E2).

-   5. Use Kübler-Ross to explain Bridges, not to override it — if the
    two genuinely disagree for more than a short period, trust whichever
    is backed by more specific, more recently gathered evidence, and
    treat the disagreement itself as worth investigating (Section
    4.2.3).

-   6. When the two clocks disagree, trust the slower one — keep
    reinforcement active on the emotional evidence's timeline, not the
    delivery plan's (Section 4.2.4 and Exception E3).

-   7. Disaggregate before every go/no-go — a single blended
    project-level reading can mask real divergence between cohorts,
    sites, or departments; check the cohort-level view before declaring
    the population ready (Exception E6).

Glossary

Lewin's Unfreeze-Change-Refreeze

Unfreeze: building the case for change and loosening attachment to the
status quo. Change: the transition itself — new processes, systems, or
behaviors take hold. Refreeze: the new state is stabilized and becomes
the organization's new normal.

Prosci ADKAR

Awareness: does the person understand why the change is happening?
Desire: do they personally want to support and participate? Knowledge:
do they know how to change? Ability: can they actually demonstrate the
new skills? Reinforcement: are there mechanisms in place to make the
change stick?

Bridges' Transition Model

Ending: letting go of the old way, often with real loss. Neutral Zone:
the in-between, where old ways are gone and new ones aren't yet
habitual. New Beginning: the new way is adopted, understood, and
starting to feel normal.

Kübler-Ross Change Curve

Denial: the change is not yet accepted as real or relevant.
Resistance/Anger: active pushback, the most visible and most misread
stage. Exploration: cautious engagement with what the change might mean.
Commitment: active, willing participation in the new way of working.
